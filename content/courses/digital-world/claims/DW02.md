# Lesson 2 (DW02, `dw-history-of-ai`): claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 1 November 2026. The Tomorrow Today headlines in the activity are made up and are not listed here, except where their feedback states a real fact.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| In 1950, Alan Turing asked, “Can machines think?” | [A. M. Turing, Computing machinery and intelligence (Oxford copy)](https://cs.ox.ac.uk/activities/ieg/e-library/sources/t%5Farticle.pdf); [Toosi and others, A brief history of AI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.01517) | Mind 59, 433–460, 1950; Toosi arXiv v3, 12 December 2022 (accepted by PET Clinics, 2021) | The paper opens: “I propose to consider the question, ‘Can machines think?’” Toosi and others date it to 1950 and link it to the imitation game (the Turing test). |
| On 31 August 1955, four researchers in the United States (McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon) wrote a proposal for a two-month, 10-person summer study of “artificial intelligence” at Dartmouth College in 1956 | [AI Magazine reprint of the proposal](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/1904) | Proposal dated 31 August 1955; reprinted in AI Magazine 27(4), Winter 2006 | “We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire.” Signed McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon. The AAAI page was blocked by bot protection; the text was read in the [Fermat's Library copy of the AI Magazine reprint](https://fermatslibrary.com/p/692449a5) and the date confirmed by [AITopics](https://aitopics.org/doc/classics:E361C990). |
| They believed every part of learning could be described well enough for a machine to copy it | Proposal (above) | 31 August 1955 | The study was to proceed “on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.” A conjecture, not a result. |
| Its topics included how a computer could use a language, and “neuron nets” | Proposal (above) | 31 August 1955 | Seven listed aspects include “How Can a Computer be Programmed to Use a Language” and “Neuron Nets”, on which “partial results have been obtained”. |
| The project itself took place at Dartmouth College in 1956 | [Dartmouth College: AI coined at Dartmouth](https://home.dartmouth.edu/about/artificial-intelligence-ai-coined-dartmouth) (claim record only); Toosi and others (in the lesson's sources) | Dartmouth page undated, read 1 November 2026; Toosi 2022 | Dartmouth: a small group met in Hanover in summer 1956, organised by John McCarthy (no exact dates or attendees). Toosi: “in the summer of 1956 … 2-month workshop at Dartmouth College”. Dartmouth's page was dropped from the lesson's sources only to keep them to six. |
| AI is one part of digital technology; it needs computers, and later used networks and digital data | Lesson framing (review section 3.1); Toosi and others (data and GPUs, below) | — | A framing sentence the review asked for, not a sourced finding. The data and chips part is sourced below. |
| Researchers tried different ideas at the same time: rules, and machines that learn from examples, like the perceptron in 1958 | [Cornell University: Professor's perceptron paved the way for AI](https://nbb.cornell.edu/news/professors-perceptron-paved-way-ai-60-years-too-soon); [Haigh, CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/?p=750773) | Cornell, 25 September 2019; Haigh, CACM 67(2), February 2024 | The US Office of Naval Research unveiled Rosenblatt's perceptron in July 1958; after 50 trials it learned to tell cards marked on the left from the right. Rule-based expert systems were a 1980s boom (Haigh). Toosi and others date the perceptron to 1957 (Rosenblatt's first report); the lesson uses the 1958 demonstration. |
| Much of the money came from governments | Toosi and others; Agar (below) | 2022; 2020 | DARPA (US) and the Science Research Council (UK) funded much early AI research. “Much” is not a measured share. |
| In 1973, a British report criticised much AI research, and Britain then cut funding for some of it | [Jon Agar, BJHS 53(3), 2020, author's copy](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10105726/3/Agar_What%20is%20science%20for,%20the%20Lighthill%20report%20and%20the%20purpose%20of%20artificial%20intelligence%20research,%20revised%20for%20BJHS.pdf) | Published 2020 (BJHS 53(3), 289–310) | The SRC asked Lighthill for a review in September 1971; he submitted it in March 1972; the SRC published it in 1973. It split AI into three areas and judged the middle one (“bridge”/robots) a failure; afterwards that area was left underfunded. Agar says the report is usually blamed for the first AI winter, but argues its motives are misread. The first draft's “Britain stopped most of its spending on AI” was stronger than this source supports. |
| In the 1980s, companies used expert systems built from experts' rules; they cost a lot to keep up, and their market fell in the late 1980s | [Thomas Haigh, How the AI boom went bust, CACM 67(2)](https://cacm.acm.org/?p=750773) | Online 25 January 2024; February 2024 issue | Expert systems drew investment and government money (Japan's Fifth Generation project, DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative); they were costly to maintain, the market “proved unsustainable”, and the bust of the late 1980s is what Haigh calls the real AI winter. |
| Times like the mid-1970s and the late 1980s are called AI winters; historians still argue about their exact dates and causes | Toosi and others; Agar; Haigh (above) | 2020–2024 | Toosi and others date the first winter from the funding cuts after ALPAC (1966) and Lighthill (1973) to the 1980s, and are themselves inconsistent about the second; Agar disputes the usual causal story; Haigh puts the deeper winter in the late 1980s. The disagreement is real. |
| In 1997, Deep Blue beat the world champion, Garry Kasparov, though it could only play chess | Toosi and others (above) | 2022 | Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov in 1997. “Could only play chess” is a description of a chess-only machine, kept from the first draft (the first draft's Centre for Computing History source, not re-opened today). |
| Machine learning began in the 1950s and grew much stronger after 2010, with large labelled photo sets and graphics chips | Cornell (above); Toosi and others (above) | 2019; 2022 | Perceptron 1958; ImageNet, “millions of labeled images”, 2009; GPUs powerful enough by 2011. |
| In 2012, a university team built a program much better at naming things in photos | Toosi and others (above) | 2022 | “In 2012 Hinton's team proposed a deep convolutional neural network architecture, named AlexNet”, described as the birth of the third AI boom. |
| Today, teams in many places build AI, including in Southeast Asia | Lesson 7 and its sources (SEA-LION, Sahabat-AI) | See DW07's claim record | A cross-reference only; the facts are sourced in Lesson 7. |
| Activity feedback: a chess computer beat the world champion in 1997, 40 years after the made-up 1957 headline | Toosi and others (above) | 2022 | 1997 is sourced; the 1957 headline is made up. |
| Activity feedback: in the late 1980s, expert systems' market fell | Haigh (above) | 2024 | As above. |
