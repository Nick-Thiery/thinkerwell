| The leaf model finds the most similar example and copies its label (nearest-neighbour); researchers studied this method in 1967 | [T. M. Cover and P. E. Hart, Nearest neighbor pattern classification](https://isl.stanford.edu/~cover/cover-papers.html) | IEEE Transactions on Information Theory IT-13(1), 21–27, January 1967 | “The nearest neighbor decision rule assigns to an unclassified sample point the classification of the nearest of a set of previously classified points.” The paper analyses the rule; it did not invent it (earlier work by Fix and Hodges, 1951, not checked here), so the lesson says “studied”. |
| Learning from labelled examples is called supervised learning, one kind of machine learning | Cover and Hart (above: “previously classified points”); [Google, Machine Learning Crash Course: Datasets, generalization and overfitting](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/overfitting) | 1967; Google page updated 27 July 2025 | Nearest-neighbour classification uses labelled (“previously classified”) examples, which is what supervised learning means. The Google unit page read today covers labels and datasets but its definition page for supervised learning could not be opened. Other kinds (unsupervised, reinforcement) are named only in a teacher note, not sourced here. |
| A machine called the perceptron learned from marked cards in 1958 | [Cornell University: Professor's perceptron paved the way for AI](https://nbb.cornell.edu/news/professors-perceptron-paved-way-ai-60-years-too-soon) | 25 September 2019 | The Office of Naval Research unveiled the perceptron in July 1958; after 50 trials it learned to tell punch cards marked on the left from those marked on the right. |
| It became far more useful after 2010, with more data and faster computers | Lesson 2's source: [Toosi and others](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.01517) | arXiv v3, 2022 | ImageNet's millions of labelled images (2009), GPUs powerful enough by 2011, AlexNet in 2012. |
| Relevant examples with correct labels help; more examples alone do not always help, because a wrong label can make guesses worse | Google, Machine Learning Crash Course (above); the activity's free play | Updated 27 July 2025 | The unit teaches that dataset quality and size matter more than a clever algorithm, lists reasons data can be unreliable, and how to improve the quality of human-rated labels. The activity shows the mechanism: label the big dark green leaf “sick” and the model gets 5 of 12 (`activities.test.tsx`). |

| A free phone app called Nuru, made by researchers from the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) and Penn State University, helps people in Tanzania check cassava leaves; it works without the internet | [IITA: African farmers get new help against cassava diseases: Nuru](https://iita.org/news-item/african-farmers-get-new-help-against-cassava-diseases-nuru-their-artificially-intelligent-assistant/) | 24 June 2018 (the page's later edit date was not checked) | Penn State “led the development of the app together with IITA”; a free Android app that recognises leaves damaged by cassava mosaic disease, cassava brown streak disease and mites; “being field tested in Tanzania”; “Nuru works offline”. It gives no accuracy figures. |
| It learned from pictures of cassava leaves taken in Tanzanian fields; in tests it did much better when it looked at six leaves from a plant than at one | [Mrisho and others, Evaluation of the accuracy of a smartphone-based artificial intelligence system, PlantVillage Nuru (bioRxiv preprint)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.01.26.919449v3.full.pdf) | 2020 preprint, version 3 (not checked whether later published in a journal) | The model was trained on leaf images of several cassava varieties grown at field sites in coastal Tanzania. Who labelled the training images and how many there were is not stated. |
