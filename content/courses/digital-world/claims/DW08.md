# Lesson 8 (DW08, `dw-when-ai-gets-it-wrong`): claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 8 November 2026 (earlier check 1 November 2026). Riverbend Learning Centre, its voice typing tool and the test results (19, 16 and 9 of 20) are made up and are not listed here.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| In a 2024 study, a widely used tool made several times as many mistakes with children's speech as with adults' | [Kid-Whisper, Attia and others (AIES 2024)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31618); read as [arXiv 2309.07927 v3 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.07927v3) | AIES 2024; v3, 15 May 2024 | Whisper models without fine-tuning: word error rate on scripted children's speech (CSLU Kids) “between 6 and 10 times” that on adult speech (Librispeech); on conversational children's speech (MyST) “between 3 and 5 times”. One tool family, particular test sets. |
| The researchers gave two reasons: far fewer recordings of children, and children's speech changes a lot as they grow | Kid-Whisper (above), introduction | 2024 | “Children's speech databases are limited and difficult to collect and transcribe”; the gap “has been attributed to inter-speaker variability due to varying developmental rates”. |
| When a tool works well for some groups and badly for others, we say it has a bias; one cause is whether the training data represents everyone who will use the tool | [Suresh and Guttag, EAAMO 2021 (arXiv 1901.10002)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10002) | EAAMO '21, October 2021 | Representation bias occurs “when the development sample underrepresents some part of the population”. The glossary's “bias” is a simplified, outcome-based definition. |
| A famous collection of more than a million photos has trained many tools; about 45% of its photos were taken in the United States and only about 2% in India; a tool trained on it was worse at naming things in photos from India or Pakistan, such as a bridegroom | Suresh and Guttag (above), section 3.2.1, citing Shankar and others | 2021 | ImageNet, 1.2 million labelled images; “approximately 45%” from the US; 1% China, 2.1% India; a classifier trained on it was “significantly worse at classifying images containing certain objects or people (such as ‘bridegroom’) when the images come from under-sampled countries such as Pakistan or India”. |
| Nobody had to plan this for the result to be unfair | Suresh and Guttag (above) | 2021 | Harm can arise from data and design choices without intent. The lesson does not claim no real system was built to exclude anyone. |
| Two computer scientists described two made-up cases: a hospital tool that missed more heart attacks in women improved with more records from women; a hiring tool that learned from unfair past ratings did not improve with more of the same data | Suresh and Guttag (above), introduction | 2021 | Introduced with “Consider the following simplified scenario”. Heart-attack model: higher false negative rate for women; more data from women improved it. Hiring model: human ratings used as labels “allowed the model to discriminate by gender”; “collecting more labelled data from the same distribution did not help.” |
| Other causes include a tool's goal, its design, or using it in a place it wasn't made for | Suresh and Guttag (above) | 2021 | Learning bias (modelling choices and objectives), aggregation, evaluation and deployment bias; “not all problems should be blamed on the data.” |
| People test a tool with many groups, look for the cause, and fix that | Suresh and Guttag (above) | 2021 | They recommend reporting results by finer subgroups and matching fixes to the source of harm. Asking families' permission before collecting children's recordings (in the Write example) is the course's rule for good practice, not a research finding. |

Experience AI (Google DeepMind and the Raspberry Pi Foundation, ages 11–14): “Bias in, bias out”, on how a limited data set can lead to bias. |
