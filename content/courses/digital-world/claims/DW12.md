# DW12 Cameras that know faces — claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 8 November 2026 (Justin's brief of 4 November). The lesson no longer has fictional material: the made-up town of Greenport (Rosa's phone, the fair, the street camera plan and its test) and the sort sentences were removed in this revision.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facial recognition finds a face, measures it, turns the measurements into a set of numbers (a template), compares templates and gives a score for how alike they are; a match is a best guess, not proof. | [NIST IR 8280, FRVT Part 3: Demographic effects](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8280.pdf); [NIST news release](https://www.nist.gov/node/1603646) | 19 December 2019 (news page updated 3 February 2025; both opened 8 November 2026) | Algorithms compare face images and return similarity scores against a threshold; one-to-one verification ("phone unlocking or passport checks") and one-to-many identification (a match against a database) are distinct; false positives and false negatives occur. "Template" is the standard term for the stored numbers. |
| A phone that opens for your face checks one face against one template, its owner's; it usually keeps only the template, not a photo. | [Apple Platform Security: Facial matching security](https://support.apple.com/guide/security/sece151358d1/web) | Apple's current guide (opened 8 November 2026) | Face ID saves "the mathematical representations of a user's face calculated during enrollment"; it discards face images captured during normal use; the data is encrypted, available only to the Secure Enclave, and "never leaves the device". One maker's design; "usually" allows for others. |
| At some airports face scans replace showing a passport; at Singapore's Changi Airport many travellers now pass the passport check by having their face and eyes scanned (evidence: since September 2024; residents on arrival and departure, visitors on departure; travellers still carry passports). | [ICA: Passport-less clearance fully rolled out at Changi Airport](https://ica.gov.sg/news-and-publications/newsroom/media-release/passport-less-clearance-fully-rolled-out-at-changi-airport) | Media release 24 October 2024 | In place at all four terminals since 30 September 2024 (trial from 5 August 2024); facial and iris biometrics; residents (citizens, permanent residents, long-term pass holders) on arrival and departure, foreign visitors on departure only; travellers should still carry passports; average clearance time cut by 60%, from 25 to 10 seconds; close to 1.5 million travellers by 15 October 2024. The release doesn't say how biometric data is stored or what legal basis applies. |
| In 2019 a US government science agency tested 189 face recognition tools; many wrongly matched two different people more often for some groups, including women, older people, children and people of some backgrounds; the best tools made far fewer mistakes. | NIST IR 8280 and news release, as above | December 2019 | 189 mostly commercial algorithms from 99 developers. Executive summary: false positive rates "often vary by factors of 10 to beyond 100 times"; "false positives to be higher in women than men"; "elevated false positives in the elderly and in children"; with application photos, highest in West and East African and East Asian people (some algorithms developed in China reversed this); the most accurate algorithms produce many fewer errors. NIST tested algorithms, not finished products, and did not test causes. |
| A wrong match in a police search could get someone blamed; cameras in public places could pick out people in a crowd without them knowing, a risk to privacy. | NIST news release (false positives in one-to-many searches can put the wrong candidate under scrutiny); [EFF Street Level Surveillance: Face recognition](https://sls.eff.org/technologies/face-recognition) | 2019; EFF page undated (from the 1 November record; not reopened) | General consequences described by NIST and EFF; the WIRED video makes the same two points. Not a statistic. |
| In 2019 San Francisco became the first US city to stop its police and other city offices from using facial recognition (evidence: airports and other places under national rules not included; security cameras still allowed). | [Al Jazeera, 15 May 2019](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2019/5/15/san-francisco-first-us-city-to-ban-facial-recognition-by-police) | 15 May 2019 (opened 8 November 2026) | The supervisors voted 8 to 1 to ban the purchase and use of facial recognition by city personnel, including police; a second vote was still needed; airports and federally regulated facilities were excluded; security cameras remained allowed; the sheriff or district attorney can appeal for exceptional use. |
| An EU law has banned some uses since February 2025: police may not use live facial recognition in public places; a few serious cases are allowed, such as a search for a missing person; a judge or another official who does not work for the police must agree first (evidence: and the country's own law must allow it). | [Bundesnetzagentur: Prohibited practices](https://bundesnetzagentur.de/EN/Areas/Digitalisation/AI/08_ProhibitedPractices/start.html); [AI Act Article 5, official text (Springlex copy of OJ L 2024/1689)](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/ai-act/ai-act-regulation/article-5/) | In place since 2 February 2025 (both opened 8 November 2026) | Art. 5(1)(h) bans real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement unless strictly necessary for: the targeted search for victims of abduction, trafficking or sexual exploitation, and for missing persons; preventing a specific, substantial and imminent threat to life or physical safety or a terrorist attack; locating or identifying suspects of listed criminal offences. Art. 5(3): each use needs "prior authorisation granted by a judicial authority or an independent administrative authority"; in urgent cases it must be requested within 24 hours. Art. 5(5): only where a member state's national law allows it; without that law, use is prohibited (Bundesnetzagentur). "Another official who does not work for the police" is a plain-words version of an independent administrative authority. |
| The law also bans building face databases by copying face photos in bulk from the internet or security cameras. | Article 5 and Bundesnetzagentur, as above | 2 February 2025 | Art. 5(1)(e) bans AI systems that build or expand facial recognition databases "through the untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage". It is limited to untargeted scraping for such databases. |
| Uses that are not banned must still follow other laws; the EU's privacy law protects face data. | [European Commission: How is data on my religious beliefs, sexual orientation, health, political views protected?](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/reform/rights-citizens/how-my-personal-data-protected/how-data-my-religious-beliefssexual-orientationhealthpolitical-views-protected_en) | Commission page (opened 8 November 2026) | Under the GDPR, "biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person" is sensitive data; processing is generally prohibited unless an exception applies, such as explicit consent or a law with adequate safeguards. This is the brief's point that "no special AI Act requirements" doesn't mean no laws apply. |
| (Teacher notes) Identifying people later from recordings is high-risk under the AI Act, with duties due from 2 December 2027 after the 2026 AI Omnibus. | [Future of Privacy Forum: The AI Act implementation timeline under the AI Omnibus](https://fpf.org/?p=250371) | 28 July 2026, updated 5 August 2026 | The Omnibus, adopted by Parliament (16 June 2026) and Council (29 June 2026) and published 24 July 2026, moves Annex III high-risk obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and adds a new prohibition (AI-generated child sexual abuse and non-consensual intimate material) from 2 December 2026; Article 5's existing bans are unchanged. That after-the-fact remote biometric identification is an Annex III high-risk use comes from the 1 November record (the Commission's overview), not reopened. |
| Singapore, by contrast, chose to use face scans at its airport. | ICA release, as above | 2024 | The government's own rollout; the lesson draws the contrast, not the source. |

**Not confirmed in this revision:** the European Commission's AI Act overview page and the AI Act Service Desk's Article 5 page could not be opened through the page reader, so the legal claims rest on the Act's text (Springlex copy), Germany's regulator and FPF; how other phone makers store face data was not checked (hence "usually"); the EFF page was not reopened; Singapore's legal basis and data-retention rules for passport-less clearance were not found. The WIRED video is unchanged; its summary was written from the transcript on WIRED's page, not from watching it.
