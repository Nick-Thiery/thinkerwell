# DW11 AI at school: help or shortcut? — claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 8 November 2026 (Justin's brief of 4 November). Brightwater School, Mira, Jonah, Ayla, the pretend tool's answers and the 1850 bridge date are made up and labelled. Brightwater's draft rules were removed in this revision.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| In 2023 UNESCO asked more than 450 schools and universities; fewer than 1 in 10 had any guidance about these tools. | [UNESCO press release, 7 September 2023 (GCED Clearinghouse copy)](https://gcedclearinghouse.org/en/node/129359) | 7 September 2023 (opened 8 November 2026) | A UNESCO survey of "over 450 schools and universities" found less than 10% had institutional policies or formal guidance on generative AI. |
| Later that year UNESCO wrote guidance for governments, asking them to protect students' privacy and to set a minimum age, such as 13, for children talking with these chat tools on their own; this is advice, not a law; each country and school makes its own rules. | [UNESCO catalogue record: Guidance for generative AI in education and research (GCED Clearinghouse)](https://gcedclearinghouse.org/en/node/129341); press release as above; [Euronews, 7 September 2023](https://www.euronews.com/next/2023/09/07/first-guide-on-ai-in-education-recommends-13-years-as-minimum-age) | September 2023 (opened 8 November 2026) | The catalogue record: the guidance recommends "mandating the protection of data privacy" and "setting an age limit for independent conversations with GenAI platforms". The press release: it "sets an age limit of 13" for AI tools in the classroom and calls for data protection and privacy standards. Euronews: it recommends 13 and notes debates about raising it to 16. It is guidance to governments, with no binding force; "each country and school makes its own rules" is the lesson's plain statement of that status. The lesson's "such as 13" reflects the difference between UNESCO's wordings. |
| In 2024 World Bank researchers tested an AI tutor in nine state schools in Benin City, Nigeria. | De Simone and others, "From chalkboards to chatbots", [World Bank working paper 11125 (copy of the PDF)](https://collimateur.uqam.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2025/05/IDU-c09f40d8-9ff8-42dc-b315-591157499be7.pdf); [reproducibility record](https://reproducibility.worldbank.org/index.php/catalog/419); [World Bank blog, 9 January 2025](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria) | Paper 20 May 2025; data collected May–July 2024 | A randomised controlled trial (reproducibility record); students "from nine public schools in Benin City" (paper); students randomly assigned to take part or not (blog). |
| The students were in their first year of senior secondary school, usually about 15 years old. | Working paper, as above | 2025 | "First-year senior secondary school students, who are typically 15 years old." |
| For six weeks they came to computer rooms after school, twice a week, to practise English with an AI chat tool. | Working paper; reproducibility record; [World Bank blog: 7 lessons](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-in-Nigeria) | 2025 | "Twelve 90-minute sessions in computer labs", at most "two 1.5-hour after-school sessions per week" (paper); Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4), English, six weeks (record); twice a week in computer labs, 800 first-year senior secondary students, Edo State, June–July 2024 (blog). Power and internet outages disrupted sessions (blog). |
| Teachers trained for the project started each session with a first question to ask the tool, then walked around helping students stay on task. | Working paper; blog: 7 lessons | 2025 | Teachers had "a single three-day training program"; sessions began with a teacher-provided prompt; teachers circulated and "played a critical role in guiding the students but did not provide direct instruction" (paper). The blog: each session began with the teacher introducing the week's topic and suggesting a starting prompt, then mentoring students, then brief reflection; monitors supervised each session. |
| At the end they did better on an English test than similar students who were not chosen. | Reproducibility record; working paper; blog, 9 January 2025 | 2025 | 0.31 SD on an assessment of English, AI knowledge and digital skills; 0.23 SD on English, the main outcome; also better end-of-year exams (blog). The comparison group were students randomly assigned not to take part. |
| The researchers say the result came from the whole programme, the tool and the teachers together. | Working paper | 2025 | The authors attribute the results to "the intervention as a whole": the interaction with the AI tool together with teacher guidance and specific prompts. The reproducibility record adds that AI tutoring works "when designed and used properly". |
| In 2023 researchers worked with nearly 1,000 students in grades 9 to 11 at a high school in Türkiye. | [Bastani and others, PNAS 122(26), abstract (IDEAS record)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [working-paper version (PDF)](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf); [Knowledge at Wharton, 27 August 2024](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education) | PNAS 2025 (doi 10.1073/pnas.2422633122); working paper 2024 | Abstract: "nearly a thousand high school math students". Working paper: a large high school in Turkey, autumn semester of 2023–24, grades 9, 10 and 11, about fifty classes (main sample 839). Wharton names Turkey. |
| In four maths lessons, a teacher reviewed a topic, students did practice problems, then took a test with no books or computers. | Working paper | 2024 | Four 90-minute sessions covering about 15% of each grade's maths curriculum: the teacher reviews a topic and solves examples; students work teacher-designed practice problems (the only randomised part); then a closed-book, closed-laptop exam with problems paralleling the practice. A teacher and a staff member were present in each session. |
| Some practised with only books; some had a chat tool that could give full answers; others a tutor version that gave hints, using answers and common mistakes written by their teachers. | Working paper; PNAS abstract | 2024; 2025 | Control: textbooks and notes. "GPT Base": a ChatGPT-like GPT-4 interface. "GPT Tutor": the same, with a prompt containing teacher-written solutions and common student mistakes, told to give incremental hints without the full answer. |
| Both AI groups did much better in practice; on the test alone the full-answer group did worse than the books-only group; the hint group about the same. | PNAS abstract; working paper | 2025; 2024 | Practice grades +48% (GPT Base) and +127% (GPT Tutor); exam without AI: GPT Base 17% lower than control; GPT Tutor no significant difference. The abstract says students used GPT Base as a "crutch". "The tool had become a shortcut" is the lesson's plain version. |
| Each study was one subject in one place, so results elsewhere may differ. | Both studies' own caveats | — | Nigeria: one subject, nine schools, six weeks, open questions on persistence and other subjects. Türkiye: one school, one subject, one model. |
| A tool can be wrong and still sound sure. | Lesson's pretend tool (activity) | — | Illustrated by the made-up 1850 bridge date, not a statistic. |
