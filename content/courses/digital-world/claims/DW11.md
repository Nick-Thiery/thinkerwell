# DW11 AI at school: help or shortcut? — claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 1 November 2026. Brightwater School, Mira, Jonah, Ayla, the draft rules, the pretend tool's answers and the 1850 bridge date are made up and labelled.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Many people use AI chat tools, including many students. | General | — | No figure is given. The first draft's "hundreds of millions" was removed because no source for it was checked. |
| In 2023 UNESCO asked more than 450 schools and universities; fewer than 1 in 10 had rules or formal guidance about AI tools. | [UNESCO survey (GCED Clearinghouse copy)](https://gcedclearinghouse.org/en/node/130058) | 2023 (kept from the first draft; not reopened) | A UNESCO survey of over 450 schools and universities found under 10% had institutional policies or formal guidance on generative AI. |
| Later in 2023 UNESCO wrote guidance for countries: protect students' privacy, and set an age limit, such as 13, for children using chat tools on their own. It is advice, not a law, and not about every learning tool. | [UNESCO catalogue record: Guidance for generative AI in education and research](https://gcedclearinghouse.org/en/node/129341); [UNESCO press release, 7 September 2023](https://gcedclearinghouse.org/en/node/129359) | Guidance and press release, 7 September 2023 | The record says the guidance proposes "mandating the protection of data privacy and setting an age limit for independent conversations with GenAI platforms". The press release says it "sets an age limit of 13 for the use of AI tools in the classroom". News reports (Euronews, Tech Xplore, 7 September 2023) say it recommends 13 and notes some want 16. It is international guidance to governments, not binding. |
| In 2024 researchers tested an AI tutor for English with secondary school students in Nigeria: six weeks after school, trained teachers guiding every session; on English tests at the end they did better than students who did not take part. | [World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11125, "From chalkboards to chatbots"](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/15e1ff08-15ae-4f7a-b2a8-d146e6c113ee/full); [World Bank blog, 9 January 2025](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria); [World Bank blog, 25 June 2025](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/addressing-the-learning-crisis-with-generative-ai--lessons-from-) | Pilot June–July 2024; paper 20 May 2025 (De Simone and others) | Randomised trial in public senior secondary schools in Edo State (Benin City); after-school sessions twice a week for six weeks (about 13 hours on average); teachers trained to open each session, guide reasoning and spot wrong answers; tested on paper for English, AI knowledge and digital skills: about 0.31 SD overall and 0.23 SD on English (the June blog says 0.24), plus better end-of-year exam results. The authors can't separate the AI from the teacher support and prompts, and don't know if effects last or work in other subjects or ordinary lessons. The tool is named Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) in the paper's abstract and ChatGPT in the June 2025 blog. |
| In a study in Türkiye, nearly 1,000 high school students practised maths; those with a chat tool giving full answers did better in practice but worse on a test without the tool; those whose tool gave hints, written with teachers' help, did about as well as students with no tool. | [Bastani and others, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning", PNAS 122(26), record on IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [Knowledge at Wharton report](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/) | PNAS 2025, doi 10.1073/pnas.2422633122; Wharton report 27 August 2024 | Field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school maths students in Türkiye (country from the Wharton report). During practice with GPT-4: plain "GPT Base" grades +48%, hint-based "GPT Tutor" (with teacher input) +127%. On an exam without AI: GPT Base 17% worse than no-AI students; GPT Tutor about the same. Shows guided practice performance is not the same as independent test results. |
| Each study was one subject in one place, so results elsewhere may differ. | Both studies' own caveats, as above | — | Nigeria: authors list open questions on other subjects, settings and persistence. Türkiye: one subject, one setting, one model. |
