| In England in the early 1800s, factory machines did work skilled cloth workers had done by hand; between 1811 and 1816 Luddites broke machines in protest. | [Britannica: Luddite](https://www.britannica.com/event/Luddite) | Britannica entry (kept from the first draft; not reopened in this revision) | The Luddite movement, 1811–16, of English textile workers who destroyed machinery they saw as threatening their work. Britannica also records harsh punishments, left out of learner text (sensitive note). |
| Cash machines spread slowly to US banks from the 1970s; a 2015 study found teller numbers stayed about the same and grew a little; banks opened more branches and tellers spent more time helping customers. | [The Conversable Economist: ATMs and a rising number of bank tellers?](https://conversableeconomist.com/2015/03/03/atms-and-a-rising-number-of-bank-tellers/), reporting James Bessen, "Toil and Technology", IMF *Finance & Development* | Blog post 3 March 2015; Bessen's article March 2015 | First US bank ATM 1969, slow growth in the 1970s, about 100,000 ATMs by 1990; teller employment roughly flat and edging up as ATMs spread; cheaper branches meant more branches (helped by looser branching rules in the 1980s–90s); tellers moved from cash handling to wider customer service. US only. |
| That was one past case, so it can't tell us what AI will do. | Lesson's own framing (review section 5, Lesson 9) | — | An interpretive caution, not a factual claim. |
| When spreadsheets came to US office computers, many bookkeepers lost work, yet the number of accountants grew. | [NPR: How the electronic spreadsheet revolutionized business](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/389585340) | 2015 (kept from the first draft; not reopened) | US example of bookkeeping clerks declining while accountants grew after spreadsheets. |
| In 2025 the ILO found about one in four jobs in the world has some exposure to generative AI ("tools that write and make things"); exposure means the tools could do some tasks, not a count of jobs lost. | [ILO: Generative AI and jobs, 2025 update](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update); [ILO news: One in four jobs at risk of being transformed by GenAI](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/one-four-jobs-risk-being-transformed-genai-new-ilo%E2%80%93nask-global-index-shows) | 20 May 2025 (Gmyrek and others; ILO with Poland's NASK) | 25% of global employment is in occupations with some degree of GenAI exposure; built from task-level scores for about 30,000 tasks; "the figures reflect potential exposure, not actual job losses". |
| Office jobs, such as clerks, have the most exposure. | ILO news, as above | 20 May 2025 | "Clerical jobs face the highest exposure of all"; exposure is rising for some digitised jobs in media, software and finance. |
| Most of these jobs are more likely to change than to disappear. | ILO publication and news, as above | 20 May 2025 | "Most jobs will be transformed rather than made redundant", because human input is still needed. |
| In 2024 the IMF warned AI could help some workers and take important tasks from others, and will probably make inequality worse unless governments act. | [IMF Blog: AI will transform the global economy](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) | 14 January 2024 (Kristalina Georgieva) | AI will replace some jobs and complement others; "in most scenarios, AI will likely worsen overall inequality", which policymakers must address. Its numbers (almost 40% of global employment exposed to AI, about 60% in advanced economies, roughly half of those likely to gain) cover AI in general, a wider scope than the ILO's generative-AI index, so they are kept in teacher notes only. |
| In rich countries the jobs most at risk of automation are 9.6% of women's jobs and 3.5% of men's (teacher notes). | ILO news, as above | 20 May 2025 | As stated. |
