# DW09 AI at work: jobs that change — claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 8 November 2026 (Justin's brief of 4 November). Millbrook (the clerks, Ms Rana, Sami) is made up and labelled. The lesson uses one estimate about AI and jobs, the ILO's; the IMF's broader 2024 figures are mentioned only in the teacher notes, with the reason they can't be compared.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| In England in the early 1800s, factory machines did work skilled cloth workers had done by hand; between 1811 and 1816 Luddites broke machines in protest, because the machines were taking their work. | [Britannica: Luddite](https://www.britannica.com/event/Luddite) | Britannica entry, opened 8 November 2026 | Organised bands of skilled English handicraftsmen who rioted against textile machinery that was displacing them; began around Nottingham in late 1811, spread in 1812, flared again in 1816. Britannica also records severe repression, including a mass trial at York in 1813 with many hangings and transportations, left out of learner text (sensitive note). |
| Cash machines spread slowly to US banks from the 1970s; a 2015 study found teller numbers stayed about the same and grew a little; each branch needed fewer tellers, so banks opened more branches; tellers spent less time handling cash and more helping customers. | [The Conversable Economist: ATMs and a rising number of bank tellers?](https://conversableeconomist.com/2015/03/03/atms-and-a-rising-number-of-bank-tellers/), reporting James Bessen, "Toil and Technology", IMF *Finance & Development* | Blog post 3 March 2015 (opened 8 November 2026); Bessen's article March 2015 | First US bank ATM 1969, slow growth in the 1970s, about 100,000 by 1990; teller employment steady and edging up; branches could be run with fewer tellers, so banks opened more (helped by looser branching rules in the 1980s–90s); tellers moved from handling checks and cash to a wider range of customer help. US only. |
| That was one machine in one country, so it can't tell us what AI will do. | Lesson's own framing | — | An interpretive caution, not a factual claim. |
| When spreadsheets came to US office computers, many bookkeepers lost work, but the number of accountants grew. | [NPR: How the electronic spreadsheet revolutionized business](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/389585340) | 2015 (opened 8 November 2026) | "Lots of bookkeepers and accounting clerks were replaced by spreadsheet software", while jobs for accountants increased, because "what-if" analysis became cheap. No figures are given. |
| In 2025 the ILO studied generative AI ("tools that write and make things"), scoring about 30,000 tasks; about one in four of the world's jobs has some exposure. | [ILO research brief: Generative AI and jobs, a 2025 update](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update); [ILO Working Paper 140](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) (read through its [web version](https://webapps.ilo.org/static/english/intserv/working-papers/wp140/index.html)) | 20 May 2025 (Gmyrek, Berg, Kamiński, Konopczyński, Ładna, Nafradi, Rosłaniec, Troszyński) | "One in four workers across the world are in an occupation with some degree of GenAI exposure"; method combines task-level data, expert input and AI predictions across nearly 30,000 tasks at the 6-digit occupation level. "Scored ... for how much of each one these tools could do" is a plain-words version of task-level automation-potential scores. |
| Exposure tells us what the tools could do; it is not a count of jobs lost. | ILO Working Paper 140, as above | 20 May 2025 | The estimates "reflect 'exposure' to GenAI and not the actual impact on employment"; they are an upper threshold, and adoption may be limited by infrastructure, skills, cost and social acceptance. |
| Office jobs, such as clerks who type in data, have the most exposure. | ILO Working Paper 140 | 20 May 2025 | Clerical occupations have the highest exposure; most of the 13 occupations in the top gradient are clerical (data entry clerks, typists and word-processing operators, accounting and bookkeeping clerks, general office clerks). |
| Most of these jobs are more likely to change than to disappear, because people are still needed for many tasks. | ILO brief and Working Paper 140 | 20 May 2025 | "Because human input remains necessary", most jobs are expected to be transformed rather than made redundant; the paper adds that efficiency gains could still reduce labour demand (teacher notes). |
| Exposure is higher in richer countries: about one in three jobs, against about one in ten in the poorest countries. | ILO Working Paper 140 | 20 May 2025 | 34% of employment in high-income countries and 11% in low-income countries is in exposed occupations. |
| In richer countries, more of women's jobs than men's are in the most exposed group. | ILO Working Paper 140 | 20 May 2025 | In high-income countries, the highest gradient covers 9.6% of female and 3.5% of male employment (globally 4.7% and 2.4%). The lesson gives no reason for the difference. |
| The ILO says talks between workers, employers and governments can help to manage these changes. | ILO research brief | 20 May 2025 | The brief calls for managing the transition through social dialogue, which it says can improve both working conditions and productivity. "Talks between workers, employers and governments" is a plain-words version of social dialogue (the ILO's term for exactly these three parties). |
| Who gains depends partly on the choices people make. | Lesson's framing, also the Vox video's argument | — | Interpretive, not a statistic. |
| (Teacher notes only) The IMF's January 2024 estimate: almost 40% of jobs exposed to AI in general. | [IMF Blog, 14 January 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) | 14 January 2024 (from the 1 November record; not reopened) | A wider scope (AI in general) and a different method from the ILO's generative-AI index, so the two numbers are not comparable; the lesson no longer quotes the IMF to learners. |

**Not confirmed in this revision:** the ILO's working paper web version was read only to about 100,000 characters, so its full policy section (beyond social dialogue and retraining) wasn't seen; Bessen's original *Finance & Development* article and US Bureau of Labor Statistics teller data weren't opened, so the teller example still rests on a summary of Bessen's research, and whether teller numbers have fallen since (for example with mobile banking) wasn't checked; the IMF blog wasn't reopened. The Vox video (2017) is unchanged and still unwatched.
