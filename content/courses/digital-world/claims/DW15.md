# DW15 What your feed shows you: claim record

Teacher-facing. Not shown to learners. Maplebrook, its vote, Ravi, Lena and every number in the evidence (900 and 60 shares, the feed counts) are made up and labelled as fiction.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| In 2018, MIT researchers published a study of about 126,000 news stories on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, each checked by fact-checking groups | [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2018/study-twitter-false-news-travels-faster-true-stories-0308) | 8 March 2018; Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, "The spread of true and false news online", Science, March 2018 | About 126,000 cascades of news stories, tweeted over 4.5 million times by about 3 million people, 2006–2017; truth judged by six fact-checking organisations that agreed over 95% of the time. Twitter gave the team access to its archive. |
| On Twitter in those years, false stories spread further and faster than true ones | Same | Same | False news was 70% more likely to be retweeted; true stories took about six times as long to reach 1,500 people. Strongest for political news. |
| False stories were often more surprising | Same | Same | Replies to false news showed more surprise and disgust; the authors' "novelty" explanation is a hypothesis, and they say they "cannot claim that novelty causes retweets". The lesson says "often more surprising", not that surprise caused the spread. |
| People shared them, not only robot accounts | Same | Same | The difference held when all bots were removed; humans drove the spread. |
| The study looked at what people chose to share on one site, not how feeds pick posts | Same | Same | It measured retweet cascades on Twitter only; the article says other platforms need separate study. It did not test a ranking algorithm, so it doesn't show that a feed or AI caused the result. |
| Feeds learn from what you watched, liked or skipped; apps aim for different things, and some ask people whether they were happy with what they saw | [YouTube Help, How YouTube recommendations work](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16089387?hl=en-GB); [CBC Kids News](https://www.cbc.ca/kidsnews/post/watch-how-do-social-media-algorithms-work) | YouTube page undated, read 1 November 2026; CBC 2021 (kept, not re-opened) | YouTube lists watch and search history, subscriptions, likes, dislikes, "not interested" and satisfaction surveys, used to understand satisfaction "not just watch time". One platform's own account, given as an example, not a description of every app. |
| A review of many studies found real filter bubbles less common than many people think | [Reuters Institute literature review](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6e357e97-7b16-450a-a827-a92c93729a08) | 2022; kept from the first draft, not re-opened | Echo chambers much less widespread than commonly assumed; little support for the filter bubble hypothesis. |
| Since 2024, an EU law says very large apps must explain how their feeds choose posts and offer one not based on what they know about you | [European Parliament Legislative Observatory, DSA summary](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1724451) | Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, adopted 19 October 2022, in force 16 November 2022, applying from 17 February 2024 | Transparency on recommender-system parameters; very large platforms (over 45 million monthly users in the EU) must offer a recommender option not based on profiling and assess systemic risks including "manipulation of elections". Very large platforms had to comply four months after designation, so earlier than February 2024 (the designation date was not confirmed here). |

The useful recommendation (Lena's free lesson on writing stories) is a made-up illustration, not a research finding.
