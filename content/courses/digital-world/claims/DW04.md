# Lesson 4 (DW04, `dw-language-models`): claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 8 November 2026 (revision for Justin's brief of 4 November). The Lakeside word game, the Lakeside helper and the book “The Long Blue Road” by Pella Raskin are made up and are not listed here.

**Source access:** on 8 November 2026 Google's Machine Learning Crash Course (the introduction to LLMs and its transformer page) and the Computer History Museum timeline were read through WebFetch. The other sources were read on 1 November and not re-opened.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| In 1966, a program called ELIZA chatted by following rules that people wrote | [Joseph Weizenbaum, ELIZA (copy at UMBC)](https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/331/papers/eliza.html) | Communications of the ACM 9(1), January 1966 | ELIZA finds keywords and applies decomposition and reassembly rules from a script that people write. The [Computer History Museum timeline](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/ai-robotics/) (read 8 November 2026) agrees: “Joseph Weizenbaum finishes ELIZA” (1966), and its DOCTOR mode “used predetermined phrases or questions and would substitute key words”. |
| Many chatbots today are built on an LLM, which uses machine learning instead | [Google for Developers, ML Crash Course: Introduction to large language models](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/llm) | Updated 9 January 2026; read 8 November 2026 | An LLM is a model trained on text that predicts tokens. “Many”, not all, chatbots. |
| An LLM learns patterns from a huge amount of text such as books, articles and web pages, much of it written by people | Google (above); [Phi-3 technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14219) (record only) | 2026; Phi-3 v1, 22 April 2024 | Phi-3's training data is “heavily filtered publicly available web data and synthetic data”: some training text is made by computers, so the lesson says “much of it”, not “all”. |
| LLMs grew from the same things as Lesson 2's photo programs: lots of digital data and fast chips (new, 8 November) | Lesson 2's sources (Toosi and others: data and GPUs); framing | 2022 | A bridge sentence. Toosi and others source data and graphics chips for the post-2010 growth of machine learning; that the same drove LLMs is a general statement, not separately sourced here. |
| In 2017, researchers published a new design for language tasks, and many later LLMs were built on it (changed, 8 November: “and many later LLMs were built on it” added) | [Vaswani and others, Attention is all you need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762); [Google ML Crash Course: LLMs, transformers](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/llm/transformers) | arXiv v1, 12 June 2017; Google page updated 2 January 2026, read 8 November 2026 | Vaswani: “We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer”, for sequence tasks such as translation. Google: “This section introduces the most successful and widely used architecture for building LLMs: the Transformer.” Confirmed; the Google page is quoted in the teacher note. |
| In November 2022, the company OpenAI released ChatGPT, a chatbot built on an LLM (changed, 8 November: “made LLMs famous” removed) | [The Centre for Computing History: OpenAI launches ChatGPT](https://computinghistory.org.uk/det/71180/OpenAI%20launches%20ChatGPT) | Read 1 November 2026 | Gives the date 30 November 2022 and OpenAI as the company. No popularity claim is made now. |
| An LLM works with tokens; a token may be a word or part of a word | Google (above) | 9 January 2026; read 8 November 2026 | “A token could be a word, a subword (a subset of a word), or even a single character.” Most modern models use subwords. |
| Worked example: it predicts a likely first token, adds it, then predicts the next using everything written so far, until the answer is finished (new example, 8 November) | Google (above) | 9 January 2026 | A language model estimates the probability of tokens to fill in what comes next. The “What is a map scale?” answer and its tokens (“A”, “map”) are an illustration, not a real model's output. |
| The cup-of-tea game is a toy example; real models do not just count words, they learn far richer patterns from far more text | Google (above); lesson framing (Justin's brief, section 11) | — | Labelled a toy analogy in the reading, evidence title, visual plan and teacher note. |
| Often each token is picked partly by chance from the likely ones; with other settings a model can give the same answer every time | Google (above) | 9 January 2026; read 8 November 2026 | The prediction “might be the highest probability” or “a random selection from tokens having a probability greater than a certain threshold”. Picking the most probable every time gives the same output for the same input. |
| LLMs are now used to help people write, translate and look for information (new, 8 November) | General statement | — | A description of common uses, not a measured or dated popularity claim. |
| Many LLMs learned mainly from English, and researchers have found they usually work worse in languages with little writing online | [Stanford Report: How AI is leaving non-English speakers behind](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/05/digital-divide-ai-llms-exclusion-non-english-speakers-research) | May 2025 (from the address) | Most major LLMs are trained mainly on English or other high-resource languages; they underperform for Vietnamese and worse for Nahuatl. “Usually” and “one big reason” (check feedback) keep it from being a full diagnosis of every system. |
| A basic LLM predicts words that sound right, and nothing in that checks the facts; it can make things up and sound confident | Google (above); the Code.org video | 2026 | A model picks likely tokens; nothing in that process checks facts. A general, stable point. |
| Some tools also search the web and show links, but the pages can be wrong or misread | [Stanford CRAFT: How do I fact-check AI search results?](https://craft.stanford.edu/resource/how-do-i-fact-check-ai-search-results) (now in the teacher note, not the learner sources) | Published 21 January 2025 | A classroom lesson on lateral reading to check what AI chatbots and AI search tools say. It does not explain how search tools retrieve pages. |
