# Lesson 4 (DW04, `dw-language-models`): claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 1 November 2026. The Lakeside word game, the Lakeside helper and the book “The Long Blue Road” by Pella Raskin are made up and are not listed here.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| An LLM works with small pieces of text called tokens; a token may be a word or part of a word | [Google for Developers, Machine Learning Crash Course: Introduction to large language models](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/llm) | Updated 9 January 2026 | Tokens can be words, subwords or single characters; most modern models use subwords. The lesson leaves out single characters to keep it to one sentence. |
| It predicts the next token, adds it, and predicts again | Google (above) | 9 January 2026 | A language model estimates the probability of tokens to fill in what comes next. |
| Often each token is picked partly by chance from the likely ones; with other settings, a model can give the same answer every time | Google (above) | 9 January 2026 | An application may pick the most probable token, or pick at random among tokens above a probability threshold. Picking the most probable token every time gives the same output for the same input. The page does not discuss repetition directly; this is the consequence of the first option. |
| An LLM learns from a huge amount of text such as books, articles and web pages; much of it was written by people | Google (above); [Phi-3 technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14219) (for why it is “much”, not “all”) | Google 2026; Phi-3 v1, 22 April 2024 | Phi-3's training data is “heavily filtered publicly available web data and synthetic data”: some training text is made by computers. The report is in this record only, to justify removing “all of this text was written by people”; the lesson makes no claim about synthetic data. |
| In 1966, a program called ELIZA chatted by following rules that people wrote | [Joseph Weizenbaum, ELIZA (copy at UMBC)](https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/331/papers/eliza.html) | Communications of the ACM 9(1), January 1966 | ELIZA finds keywords and applies decomposition and reassembly rules from a script that people write; “a script is data”. |
| LLMs use machine learning instead; in 2017 researchers published a new design for language tasks | [Vaswani and others, Attention is all you need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) | arXiv v1, 12 June 2017 | “We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer”, for sequence tasks such as translation. That later LLMs are built on it is common knowledge but not stated by this source; the lesson only says a new design was published. |
| ChatGPT was released on 30 November 2022 and made LLMs famous | [The Centre for Computing History: OpenAI launches ChatGPT](https://computinghistory.org.uk/det/71180/OpenAI%20launches%20ChatGPT) | Read 1 November 2026 | Gives the date 30 November 2022. “Made them famous” is a general description, not a measured claim. The first draft's “fastest-growing app ever” (an analyst estimate reported by Reuters, February 2023) was removed. |
| A basic LLM does not check facts; it can make things up and sound confident | Google (above); the Code.org video (summary) | 2026 | A model picks likely tokens; nothing in that process checks facts. A general, stable point rather than a measurement. |
| Some tools also search the web and show links, but the pages can be wrong or misread, so you still need to check them | [Stanford CRAFT: How do I fact-check AI search results?](https://craft.stanford.edu/resource/how-do-i-fact-check-ai-search-results) | Published 21 January 2025 | A classroom lesson (History–Social Science) on lateral reading to check what AI chatbots and AI search tools say; “AI makes mistakes”. It does not explain how search tools retrieve pages. |
| Most big LLMs learned mainly from English text and work worse in languages with little writing online | [Stanford Report: How AI is leaving non-English speakers behind](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/05/digital-divide-ai-llms-exclusion-non-english-speakers-research) | May 2025 (from the address; the page shows no date) | Most major LLMs are trained mainly on English or other high-resource languages; they underperform for Vietnamese and worse for Nahuatl; Swahili has 200 million speakers but too few digitised resources. The Swahili figure was removed from this lesson (Lesson 7 has it). |
