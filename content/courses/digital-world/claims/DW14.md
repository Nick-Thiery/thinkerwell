# DW14 Checking a claim before you share it: claim record

Teacher-facing. Not shown to learners. Brightwater, its bridge and every source card are made up and labelled as fiction.

|---|---|---|---|
| In 2017, researchers at Stanford watched 45 people judge real websites about social issues: 10 historians who teach at universities, 10 professional fact-checkers and 25 university students | [Wineburg and McGrew, working paper](https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/Teaching_and_Learning/EvaluatingDigitalInformation.pdf); [SSRC MediaWell summary](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/citations/lateral-reading-reading-less-and-learning-more-when-evaluating-digital-information/) | Stanford History Education Group Working Paper 2017-A1; shortened version in Teachers College Record 121 (November 2019) | 10 PhD historians on the faculty of four-year colleges or universities in California or Washington; 10 staff of news and political fact-checking organisations; 25 first-year Stanford undergraduates. They evaluated live websites and searched the web, thinking aloud, on timed tasks: two doctors' groups' articles on bullying, who was behind minimumwage.com, and who funded a teacher-tenure lawsuit. |
| Most historians and students stayed on one page and read it from top to bottom; official-looking pages fooled them | Same | Same | Historians and students tended to read vertically (staying on one site) and were often misled by "official-looking logos and domain names". Not every one: for example six historians and 40% of students traced the minimum-wage site's backers. Hence "most" in the lesson. |
| Fact-checkers left the page quickly, opened other sources, and reached better answers in much less time | Same | Same | Fact-checkers read laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan and opening new tabs, and reached better-supported conclusions in much less time (paraphrase of the abstract). |
