# Lesson 7 (DW07, `dw-ai-and-languages`): claim record

Teacher-facing; not shown to learners. Checked 1 November 2026. Harbourside, Languages A, B and C, the help screen and its results (19, 15 and 6 of 20) are made up and are not listed here.

| Claim | Source (link) | Date or version | What the source actually establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most big AI tools learned mainly from English | [SEA-LION paper (arXiv 2504.05747)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05747); [Stanford Report](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/05/digital-divide-ai-llms-exclusion-non-english-speakers-research) | v4, 30 October 2025; 2025 | “Most LLMs remain heavily English-centric” (SEA-LION paper, citing others); Stanford: major models work well in English and worse in languages with less data. |
| People speak thousands of languages | Civic Tech Guide on Masakhane (below) | Undated listing | Around 2,000 of the world's languages are African, so “thousands” is safe. The earlier “more than 7,000” was dropped: Ethnologue could not be opened. |
| A low-resource language has little digital material computers can use for a task; the name is about data, not the language or its speakers | Stanford Report (above); [Kid-Whisper (arXiv 2309.07927)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07927) | 2025; AIES 2024 (v3, 15 May 2024) | Stanford: “languages with limited amounts of computer-readable data”. Kid-Whisper calls children's speech recognition “a low resource task”, showing the term is about data for a task. “Not about worth” is the lesson's framing, consistent with the definition. |
| About 200 million people speak Swahili, but there is not enough Swahili writing online for AI to learn well | Stanford Report (above) | 2025 | About 200 million speakers; not enough digitised material for AI training. |
| How much writing a language has online is linked to history and power; Masakhane links the small place of African languages in technology partly to colonialism | [Civic Tech Guide: Masakhane](https://civictech.guide/projects/masakhane) | Undated listing | Masakhane's description: the colonial past damaged the support and integration of African languages in technology. This is Masakhane's view, presented as such. |
| AI Singapore, a government-funded programme, builds SEA-LION models | SEA-LION paper (above); [Context](https://www.context.news/ai/singapore-builds-ai-model-to-represent-southeast-asians) | v4, 30 October 2025; updated 8 February 2024 | Authors from AI Singapore (National University of Singapore) and NTU; funded by the National Research Foundation's National Large Language Models Funding Initiative. Context: built under a government-backed project. |
| A 2025 paper says they support 11 languages used in the region | SEA-LION paper (above) | v4, 30 October 2025; models Llama-SEA-LION-8B-IT and Gemma-SEA-LION-9B-IT | “Supports 11 SEA languages, namely English, Chinese, Indonesian, Vietnamese, Malay, Thai, Burmese, Lao, Filipino, Tamil, and Khmer.” The earlier draft's “11 Southeast Asian languages such as Thai and Vietnamese” (from Context) left out that English and Chinese are among the 11. |
| Learning from a language is not the same as working well in it; the team's main tests covered only four | SEA-LION paper (above), Limitations and section 4.2 | v4, 30 October 2025 | SEA-HELM “only covers four languages” (Indonesian, Vietnamese, Thai, Tamil in the reported tables); SEACrowd covers 31 languages but only classical tasks, not chat. Scores differ by language (for example Tamil lower on several measures). |
| The first SEA-LION model's training text was about 63% English; critics' concerns | Context (above) | Updated 8 February 2024 | About 63% English, 13% Southeast Asian languages, over 9% Chinese; critics warned of biased or revisionist narratives. (Teacher notes only.) |
| In Jakarta in November 2024, two companies launched Sahabat-AI for Bahasa Indonesia, Javanese and Sundanese | [GoTo press release](https://gotocompany.com/en/news/press/goto-launches-sahabat-ai-enhancing-its-leadership-in-indonesias-technology-sector) | 15 November 2024 (dateline Jakarta, 14 November) | Co-developed by GoTo and Indosat Ooredoo Hutchison; 8B and 9B models; Bahasa Indonesia, Javanese and Sundanese, with Batak and Balinese planned; open source on Hugging Face. |
| Its makers said it beat similar models, but was at an early stage | GoTo press release (above) | 15 November 2024 | “Demonstrated leading performance in Bahasa Indonesia, Javanese and Sundanese, outperforming other open-source models with a similar number of parameters”, with no published scores in the release; “still in the early stage of development”. A developer's claim, not an independent test. |
| Masakhane's name means “We build together” in isiZulu; its members build tools for African languages with the people who speak them | Civic Tech Guide (above) | Undated listing | Name roughly translates as “We build together”; open, participatory, community-built research for African languages. |
| Speakers can help a tool fit their community, but people still need to test it | Stanford Report; SEA-LION paper (above) | 2025 | Stanford: some communities build their own models; the SEA-LION paper says benchmarks don't yet cover all languages. A general conclusion, not a measured result. |

Not confirmed: AI Singapore's own SEA-LION website and model cards (sea-lion.ai, Hugging Face, GitHub) could not be opened, so the first release date of SEA-LION is not given in the lesson. Sahabat-AI's later versions and languages were not checked.
