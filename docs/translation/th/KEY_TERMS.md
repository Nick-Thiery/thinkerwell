# Thai: key terms

Drafted by AI (November 2026), for the AI translators of the interface, the four courses' lessons and section checks, and the picture labels. These words come up again and again. Every translator uses the Thai in this file for the term, so the whole platform uses one word for one idea. If you think a word here is wrong, use it anyway and flag the string in your notes file: one native speaker then decides, and the change is made everywhere at once.

Terms marked **(check)** were hard to choose; a native speaker should look at them first. Everything in this file is open to the native-speaker review.

## House style (built into the checker: keep it exactly)

- **Voice.** Write the way a kind, clear teacher talks to a 12-year-old: modern standard Thai as used in Thai schools and apps, everyday words, short sentences. Prefer an everyday word to a formal Pali or Sanskrit one where one exists (เริ่ม, not ริเริ่ม; ใช้, not ประยุกต์ใช้). Where the course teaches a term (a key word), use the term in this file and let the glossary explain it.
- **The learner is คุณ.** Thai often drops the pronoun: do that wherever the sentence stays clear ("เลือกหนึ่งคำตอบ", not "คุณเลือกหนึ่งคำตอบ"). **When the learner speaks** (self-check items, "I'm new here", the "I practised ..." choices, planning-box labels such as "A question I still have"), the learner is **ฉัน**.
- **Never กรุณา or โปรด** for "please", **never ท่าน**, on any page, the adults' pages included (teachers and organisations are คุณ too). The checker matches these letters inside other words, so also avoid ความกรุณา (use ความใจดี), โปรด as "favourite" (use ที่ชอบที่สุด) and words that begin ท่าน… such as ท่าน้ำ or ท่านั่ง (use ท่าเรือ; rephrase the other).
- **No gendered polite particles** in anything a learner reads: no ครับ, ค่ะ, คะ, นะคะ, จ้ะ. Neutral phrasing carries the politeness. นะ is fine, rarely.
- **Instructions are plain commands** ("อ่านส่วนนี้", "เขียน 4 ถึง 6 ประโยค"), never exam-style จง. ลอง… softens one that sounds blunt ("ลองอ่านอีกครั้ง"). Don't start every instruction with ให้.
- **No exclamation marks.** Anywhere.
- **No full stops.** Thai ends a sentence with a single space: "ประโยคแรก ประโยคที่สอง". The checker counts a "sentence" as a run between spaces, so put a space between sentences and between clauses where a Thai writer would, and nowhere else. Keep a question mark at the end of a direct question (common in Thai school books and apps, and it helps learners see a question) **(check)**. Thai uses no commas between words: separate list items with a space and put และ or หรือ before the last ("ประวัติศาสตร์ ภูมิศาสตร์ และวัฒนธรรม"). Use curly double quotes “…”. Write ๆ joined to its word ("เด็กๆ", "ต่างๆ"), not ฯลฯ (write และอื่นๆ).
- **Sentence starters keep their ending:** if the English ends in "..." or "___", so does the Thai ("ฉันคิดว่า...", "หลักฐานที่สำคัญที่สุดคือ ___").
- **Quick-check and section-check feedback** starts with **ถูกต้อง** (the right answer) or **ยังไม่ถูก** (a wrong answer), then a space, then the reason and the hint: "ถูกต้อง ใบเสร็จบอกว่า..." / "ยังไม่ถูก ลองดูวันที่บนใบเสร็จอีกครั้ง". A wrong answer is never called ผิด. Keep the options as clearly right or wrong as the English, and don't make the right one longer.
- **Self-check items start with ฉัน** ("ฉันใช้หลักฐานจากบทเรียนอย่างน้อยหนึ่งอย่าง").
- **The completion message starts คุณเรียนจบบทเรียนที่ N แล้ว** (N is the lesson number), then a space and the rest.
- **Numbers:** Arabic digits, never Thai digits (๑ ๒ ๓). Thousands with a comma (1,000; 25,000), decimals with a point (1.5). **Years are never converted to พ.ศ.**: keep the English year (the checker compares the numbers). Write "ในปี 1945"; a year above 2,400 would be พ.ศ., so a reader sees a CE year at once. Years take no comma (1458, not 1,458). "1458 BCE" is "ปี 1458 ก่อนคริสต์ศักราช"; "700 CE" is "ปีคริสต์ศักราช 700"; "the 1980s" is "ช่วงทศวรรษ 1980"; "the 1400s" is "ช่วงปี 1400 ถึง 1499" **(check)**. Spell out units and symbols, because the text is read aloud: 8 เปอร์เซ็นต์ (not %), กิโลเมตร, กิโลกรัม, องศาเซลเซียส, and no ค.ศ., พ.ศ. or other abbreviations (กรุงเทพฯ is the one exception).
- **Keep `{placeholders}` and `<tags>` exactly** (don't translate the word in the braces); move them to where they belong in the Thai. In a message with number forms (`one`, `other`), Thai has only **`other`**: give `{"other": "..."}` with `{count}` in it.
- **Short labels stay short** (buttons, tabs, menu links): at most about 1.5 times the English. A phone is narrow.
- **Names:** "Thinkerwell", people's names and invented places (Riverstone, Tallgrass Valley, Bayview, Riverlands ...) stay in Latin letters exactly as they are. Real places take their usual Thai name (list below). AI, YouTube, VOC and other Latin names stay Latin.
- **Thinkerwell is a แพลตฟอร์ม**, never โครงการ (project), มูลนิธิ (foundation) or องค์กรการกุศล (charity): it is student-led and not registered as a charity or nonprofit. "social studies" is สังคมศึกษา; "youth" is เยาวชน.
- **Sensitive subjects.** Lesson 4 (how faiths and the sciences explain where the world comes from): use each faith's own usual Thai name (list below), keep every account respectful, equal and unranked, and flag the whole lesson for a reviewer from each faith it names. Lessons 1 and 23 (judging sources) and 24 (contributing to a community): translate exactly, add no example from Thailand or anywhere the English does not name.
- **Kings and royal words.** Use the usual Thai school forms for rulers (กษัตริย์, ครองราชย์) without piling up ราชาศัพท์; keep it plain and the same for every country's rulers.
- **Key words in the lessons.** Each glossary word must appear, exactly as written in `read.glossary[].word`, in both the standard and the simpler text of a reading part. Thai has no spaces between words, so the checker only looks for the letters: no `forms` are needed unless the reading uses a different word.
- **Simpler text** is clearly shorter: short sentences (the checker warns above 12 words on average, or any sentence over 20), the commonest words, one idea per sentence.
